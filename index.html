LOOM

A two-track effects chain for the NLM Xenomorpher. It also runs on standard O.R.N.8 hardware. Patch in your voices, drums or anything else from the rack: each track takes a sound, threads it through four effects and an output stage, and moves it with modulators and a clock. This covers LOOM RC1 (release candidate 1), build 4704.

Download the firmware (LOOM-RC1_4704.hex) and flash it with the Teensy Loader: open the file, connect the module over USB and press the program button on the Teensy. A module that already runs LOOM doesn't need the button: use FLASH on the clock page instead. Release notes and earlier builds are on the releases page.

The weaving words

Where LOOM's names come from, and what each one means here

Warp and weft are the two sets of threads in woven cloth. The warp threads are stretched lengthwise on the loom and held still, under tension. The weft is drawn across them, over and under, from side to side. The warp carries the tension, and the weft carries almost none. The names are old: weft comes from the Old English wefan, “to weave”, and warp from weorpan, “to throw”. The thrums are the loose warp ends left on the loom when the cloth is cut off; the shuttle carries the weft back and forth. In everyday speech, the “warp and weft” of something is what it is made of.

LOOM names its parts after the loom. Each track is a thread of sound, and the devices wind it, carry it, stretch it and weave it into cloth. Once you know what the words mean on a real loom, you can guess what the controls do:

Overview

Two tracks, their slots, and the weave map of every device

LOOM runs on the NLM Xenomorpher and on standard O.R.N.8 hardware. The controls, screens and jacks described here are the same on both.

LOOM needs a Teensy 4.1 with PSRAM (the extra memory chips). 8 MB (the usual Teensy 4.1 setup) is enough: REPEAT, FIBRE and DRAFT take their fixed share, and loops and samples share the rest, each taking only the length it really holds, up to 16 s. Without PSRAM, SPOOL, SHUTTLE, FIBRE, DRAFT and REPEAT can't run. The boot screen shows the version, RC1, for 2 s (set how long with BootScreen on the clock page), or a blinking NO PSRAM! when there is none, which stays until you touch a button or knob. The version and build number are on the clock page and the card mode page.

LOOM has two tracks, A and B. Each track is a chain of six slots, played left to right as in the weave view. Each slot holds one of the devices stacked in its column:

Each track also has four modulators of its own (A–D), and both tracks share four more (E–H). Any setting can be moved by up to two of them. The eight CV jacks can output any modulator, so LOOM can also drive the rest of your system.

In the rack. The two audio inputs take any line or modular level: an oscillator after its VCA, a drum module, a mixer's send. Envelopes and gates shape what LOOM does with it. A sharp attack makes BEATER and REED ring, a sequence with rests leaves room for the echoes and grains, and the FOLLOW modulator turns the level coming in into movement. The trigger inputs, and the CV inputs used as gates, can press any device's actions (freeze, hit, record), and can run the clock from your sequencer. The eight CV outputs send the modulators and the clock back out to the rest of the rack.

The weave view

The home screen: both tracks' chains at a glance; when the processor is full

The weave view is LOOM's home screen. It shows both tracks as two rows of boxes, each box one device in the chain, in the order the sound passes through them. Every box draws a small live picture of what its device is doing: a loop going round, a file playing, the level coming in. So you see the whole set at a glance.

Here you build the chain: put devices in the slots, change their order, bypass them, and open their pages. The bottom line names the device under the cursor and shows the level coming in.

How to play it

  • Turn R to move along track A, then on to track B.
  • Turn L to put another device in the slot. On CLOTH it sets the output level.
  • Push R to open the device's page. Y brings you back. On a slot with no device, R brings back the device it had, bypassed, so you can set it up before you hear it.
  • Hold R and turn on an effect to move it along the chain, for example REPEAT before TEXTURE.
  • Press B to bypass the slot. On SHUTTLE, press A to start it and hold A for 2 s to stop it.
  • Hold Y for 2 s to save everything (SAVE STATE).
More about The weave view

With the cursor on INPUT or SPOOL, the right end of the bottom line shows the level coming in, in dB (for example -08dB). When the input jack is driven too hard, CLIP shows there in a white box, whichever slot the cursor is on: turn the level down at the source. INPUT's own page says IN CLIP (the jack) or GAIN CLIP (GAIN turned up too far).

  • R turn moves the cursor along track A, then on to track B.
  • L turn changes the device in the slot. On CLOTH (CLO) it sets the output level, up to 200.
  • R push opens the device's page. On a slot with no device in it, R brings back the device it last had, bypassed, and opens its page: set it up in silence, then press B to bring it in.
  • Hold R and turn on an effect to move it along the chain. This is how you put REPEAT before TEXTURE, for example.
  • B bypasses the slot.
  • Changing a slot's device or bypassing it fades over a quarter of a second, so a device glides in and out instead of jumping. The same goes for CLOTH's page 2.
  • A does nothing to the effects here, so a set never stops or changes by a stray press: freezing, hits and recording are on each device's own page. On SHUTTLE, a single press starts it. Once it plays, hold A for 2 s to stop it: a STOP... bar fills while you hold.
  • Hold Y for 2 s to save everything: SAVE STATE fills while you hold, then STATE SAVED. On a device's page the same hold saves just that device (see saving).

When the processor is full

LOOM can't run every effect on both tracks with every setting turned up at once. It looks after this itself, so the sound never breaks up:

  • CPU FULL. Before an effect comes in (B on a bypassed slot, a device picked with L, BEATER's sympathetic models, linking A and B to TIGHT), LOOM checks that it still has room for it with all its settings turned up. If not, nothing changes and CPU FULL shows for a moment. Bypass or change another effect to make room. Anything LOOM lets in can be turned up all the way afterwards.
  • Lighter when busy. When the load goes over 85 %, the heavy effects work lighter until it has been under 65 % for 3 s: REPEAT's reverb runs on 4 of its 8 lines, REED leaves out bands that are barely heard, TWILL lets ringing notes stop sooner and FIBRE makes at most 5 grains at a time.
  • Last resort. If the processor is ever completely swamped, LOOM leaves out a few milliseconds of sound instead of freezing.

Linking A and B

THREAD, LOOSE and TIGHT: the two tracks apart, in a row, or moving as one

The two tracks can run on their own or be linked. In THREAD, track A runs on into track B, one long chain over two rows. In LOOSE and TIGHT, the tracks keep their own sound but share the knobs, so a stereo pair or a doubled part moves as one.

A short label pops up when you change the link, and a small sign between the A and B letters on the left shows the mode afterwards. Nothing is lost on the way: B's own settings wait while it follows A, and come back when you leave.

How to play it

  • Press A+B together to step through FREE, THREAD, LOOSE and TIGHT.
  • In THREAD, put effects on track B to process track A further.
  • In LOOSE, set B a little different from A first: the offsets stay as you turn.
  • In TIGHT, B copies A exactly, both in the centre, one row to edit.
More about Linking A and B
ModeWhat happens
FREETwo separate stereo tracks. Everything is independent.
THREADTrack A's output becomes track B's input, so B processes A. Both are heard. With WARP on track B and its THRU on, A's sound runs through WARP, under its notes, and on through B's effects.
LOOSETurning a knob on A turns the same knob on B by the same amount. B keeps its own offsets.
TIGHTB copies A exactly. Both tracks are panned to the centre, and the cursor stays on one row. B's own settings are kept aside meanwhile: when you leave TIGHT, B gets them back, so going round the modes loses nothing.

Track B can also take track A's output in its INPUT device (source OUT A) without linking. There is no feedback route from B back to A. If you want feedback, patch it with cables.

Device pages

Every device page works the same way: settings, modes, variations

Every device has a page of its own, and every page works the same way. The top line names the track and the device and shows its two actions. The middle is a live picture of what the device is doing to the sound. At the bottom sit four settings, names above and values below, on one or two pages.

Each setting has two sides. The R knob sets its value. The L knob sets its mode: another way of working, a zoom for the picture, or a variation that moves the setting around your value with every beat, so the sound keeps changing on its own.

How to play it

  • Turn R to move between the settings, and on to page 2.
  • Push R to grab a setting, turn R to change it, push R again to let go. Turn slowly for single steps, spin faster to cover a wide range quickly.
  • Turn L right for the setting's mode or variation; turn it back all the way left and its name returns.
  • Push R twice quickly to put a setting and its mode back to their defaults, with its name on top. Again, and everything comes back.
  • Press A and B for the device's actions. On an effect, B bypasses it.
  • Hold L and let go for the trigger page: a gate, CV input, MIDI note or modulator that presses the actions for you.
  • Hold R for 2 s to reset the whole device. Hold L for 2 s right after to undo it.
  • Hold Y for 2 s to save just this device: the rest stays as it was last saved.
More about Device pages
  • R turn moves between settings. After the fourth one, it goes on to the next page: a device has one or two pages of four (the top right shows which, for example 2/2).
  • A device opens again on the setting you left it on: REPEAT on its reverb page if that is where you were, and so on.
  • R push grabs the setting. Now R turn changes the value. Push again to let go.
  • Push R twice quickly to put a setting back to its default; do it again to get your value back. A few settings do something handier instead: SPOOL's SPEED reverses and its MON switches between 100 and 0, SHUTTLE's SPEED goes to 100 % (with SYNC: the clock's tempo), FADE goes to 0, and RANDOM's VARI goes to 0, which freezes its pattern. The settings that pick the input or the file are left alone.
  • L turn changes the mode of the setting, if it has one. A setting that has modes reads its own name in the upper row, so you see what it is for. Its name is the first stop of the L knob, left of the first mode: one click right shows the mode it is on without changing it, and further clicks step through the modes, so SHUTTLE's SPEED reads FWD, REV or SYNC, a RATE FREE or CLOCK, a variation ±35 %, TWILL's ROTA its MORPH time. Turn L back past the first mode and the name returns. A double click on R puts the value and the mode back to their defaults and the name back in the upper row. START and END on SPOOL and SHUTTLE, and INPUT's VIEW, keep their names, because their L knob only zooms the picture. The value row shows the number with its unit: 89%, 746ms, +4dB, +12st, 250Hz, 7.09s, PAN as L30, C or R30.
  • Variations. Many settings have a variation on the L knob: OFF, then ±5 % to ±100 % of the setting's range. On every beat of the clock the setting moves to a new random spot that far above or below where you put it, and glides there, so LEVEL, PAN, CUT, DRIVE and the like wander with the music. The number below always shows your setting; the sound and the picture follow the variation. (DRAFT, FIBRE's grains and REED / TWILL's hits draw theirs for each window or hit instead.)
  • A setting that has modes shows a thin line over its name, cut into as many parts as it has modes. The part for the mode it is on is drawn: with two modes the left or the right half, with three the left, middle or right third, and with many modes (a variation from OFF to 100 %) a short piece that moves along as you turn. A zoom (START and END on SPOOL and SHUTTLE) grows from the middle outwards, the wider the closer it looks. TWILL's SPRD has as many parts as DIR's bells allow. On REPEAT's ROOM and WALL the two halves are the setting and its second value (GLINT, PRSET).
  • Hold L (and let go) to open the trigger page. It sets what fires the device's two actions: a trigger or CV input, a MIDI note, or one of the modulators MOD A to H (see TREADLE). Each knob sets one action. A modulator that has its own trigger setting shares it with this page, so the two always show the same source: ADSR's TRG is its TRIG, and the RST of WAVE and TABBY is their RESET. While it is open, nothing else reacts. Push L or press Y to close it.
  • Hold R for 2 s to put the device back to its default settings. A RESET... bar fills while you hold, then DEFAULTS shows. Let go early, or turn a knob, and nothing happens. The device keeps what is routing rather than sound: SHUTTLE keeps its file, WARP its source and thrum, INPUT and SPOOL their input, CLOTH the track's pan.
  • Changed your mind? Right after a reset, hold L for 2 s (an UNDO... bar fills) and the device gets back everything it had. The undo is there until you leave the device's page.
  • Y goes back to the weave view.

Modulating a setting

A modulator, CV input or MIDI on any setting; pitch from MIDI

Any setting on any page can be moved by up to two modulators at once: a wave, an envelope, a random pattern, a follower, a drum pattern, a clock divider, or a MIDI controller. They add up around the value you set, so the setting is the middle and the modulators swing it from there.

A modulated setting shows how far it can move as a line under its value, with a tick where it is right now, so you can see the movement even without hearing it. Pitch settings can also follow the notes of a MIDI keyboard.

How to play it

  • Put the cursor on a setting and push L: MOD1 shows at the top.
  • Turn L to pick the modulator, A to H, moving the setting one way (u) or both ways (b).
  • Turn R to set how much, up to ±100 %.
  • Push R for MOD2, a second modulator on the same setting.
  • Push L again to leave.
  • In MOD1, hold X to learn a knob from your MIDI controller.
More about Modulating a setting
  1. Put the cursor on a setting and push L. The top line shows MOD1.
  2. L turn picks the modulator (A–H) and whether it moves the setting one way (u) or both ways (b). From off, the first click to the right goes straight to the modulator you adjusted last, as u or b, whichever you picked last time. A fast turn stops there too. Turn on from there for the others.
  3. R turn sets the amount, from −100 % to +100 %.
  4. R push switches to MOD2, a second modulator for the same setting.
  5. Push L again to leave.

A modulated setting shows how far it can move as a line under its value, with a moving dot where it is right now. In MOD1, hold X to MIDI-learn the setting to a controller.

“The modulator you adjusted last” is the one whose page you last turned a knob on, or whose type you last changed in the MOD view. LOOM remembers it, and its u or b, with the saved settings.

Playing a pitch from MIDI

Every pitch setting (FIBRE's, REED's and DRAFT's PITCH, BEATER's NOTE) has one more source: MIDI. In MOD1 or MOD2, turn the L knob one click left from off: it reads MIDI, and the amount goes to +100 % by itself. The setting now follows the last MIDI note played, on any channel: C3 (note 60) leaves it as it is, each key above or below moves it a semitone. At −100 % the keyboard runs upside down. The last note stays when you let go. Turn right again to get back to off.

With a RANDOM on MOD1 and MIDI on MOD2, the random melody is transposed by the key you hold.

INPUT

The jacks: what comes in from the rack

INPUT is the plain way in: the sound at the track's audio jacks, straight into the chain. It takes any line or modular level, in stereo or from one side only, and on track B it can also take track A's output, so B can process A without linking the tracks.

Its picture is a small oscilloscope. It shows the sound as it arrives, from a few cycles of a single note up to whole phrases, so you can see at a glance that something is coming in and how hard.

How to play it

  • Turn SOURC to pick the jacks: both, left, right, or off. On track B, OUT A takes track A's output.
  • Turn GAIN until the level box moves well but CLIP never shows.
  • Turn WIDTH down for mono, past 100 to spread a stereo source wider.
  • Turn VIEW short to see the shape of a wave, long to see rhythm and how each hit dies away.
  • Press A (MUTE) to fade the input out and back in. Press B (DUCK) for a quick dip, like a kick pumping it: give it a gate on the trigger page to pump in time.
More about INPUT

SHUTTLE

Plays and loops sound files from the card

SHUTTLE plays sound files from the SD card: drum loops, field recordings, whole songs, your own saved loops and takes. It can stream any length straight from the card, or load a loop of up to 16 seconds into memory, where it can also play backwards.

It finds the tempo of a file by itself, so it can play in time with the clock, and each file remembers its own loop points and settings. Pick a file again weeks later and it comes back the way you left it.

How to play it

  • Push R on MODE to open the file list. Turn either knob to scroll, push R to open a folder or load a file, press Y to close the list.
  • In the list, press B for a quick listen, and hold B for AUTO: every file the cursor stops on plays by itself.
  • Press A to play, B (TOP) to go back to the start.
  • Hold B for 2 s to send the loop to SPOOL: a TO SPOOL bar fills, then the track’s SOURCE turns to SPOOL and the loop plays on from where SHUTTLE was.
  • Turn SPEED's L knob to SYNC to play at the clock's tempo. SPEED then sets half time, double time and so on.
  • Pull in START and END for a loop, add FADE to smooth the seam, and set SNAP to BEAT to keep the loop on the beat.
  • With SNAP on BEAT, turn the L knob of START or END to pick the grid: BEAT, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 or 1/16. Each click of START or END then moves one step of that grid.
  • Put the cursor on SNAP and turn its L knob to move the whole loop a few milliseconds earlier or later, for example past a silent start. The picture shows the loop's start and end close up while you do.
  • For an exact tempo, set SNAP to ZERO, set START and END by ear around a few bars (on the kick, for example), then turn SNAP back to BEAT. The loop stays, and the tempo is taken from it.
More about SHUTTLE

In the file list, “..” at the top goes up a folder. The quick listen plays the file under the cursor once, straight from the card and past all effects, so you can listen through a folder without changing what SHUTTLE plays. Loops in SPOOLS play just their loop section, as set in SPOOL; other files play whole. B again stops it, and so does closing the list. A small arrow marks the file you are hearing. Hold B again, or close the list, to turn AUTO off.

The top line shows the file's tempo while SHUTTLE is stopped, and the position in the file (minutes, seconds, tenths) while it plays, so pressing A switches between them. The tempo is found when the file loads, between 70 and 180 BPM. A streamed file (STRM) is read for about 30 s from the loop's START. A file in memory (MEM) is read as the loop it is, START to END and round again, so loops down to about 1.5 s get a tempo too. It is shown as you hear it, so SPEED changes it. The number is in BPM; - means no clear beat was found. A tempo taken from a loop you set by hand (see SNAP) replaces the one found, and stays with the file.

Each file keeps its own settings. When you pick a file, it comes back with the loop points, LOOP, LEVEL, SPEED, FADE and SNAP you last used with it. A file you have never used starts with the whole file as the loop: START at its beginning, END at its end. They are saved in a small file with the same name next to the WAV (for example drums.loom next to drums.wav) when you leave the SHUTTLE page, pick another file, or save. The sound file itself is never changed. Both tracks can still play the same file with different settings: each track keeps its own, and the file's settings are only where a fresh pick starts.

SPOOL

A live looper: record, overdub, trim

SPOOL is a live looper at the start of the chain. It records what comes in, up to 16 seconds, and plays it round and round while you overdub, trim it, slow it down or turn it backwards. Everything after it in the chain works on the loop, so a phrase you play once becomes material for the effects to chew on.

It only holds memory for what you record: the loop keeps just its own length and gives the rest back. A loop can be saved to the card and opened in SHUTTLE, SHUTTLE can hand its loop over to SPOOL, and a WARP on the other track picks it up by itself, so you can play it from a keyboard straight away.

How to play it

  • Press A to record. Press B to close the loop and play it.
  • Press A on a playing loop to overdub, and again to go back to just playing. MON's L knob (DUB) sets how much of the old loop stays.
  • Press B to stop and start the loop. Hold B for 3 s to clear it.
  • Pull in START and END to loop just a part. Turn their L knob to zoom in on the edge you move.
  • Set SNAP to ZERO for a quiet seam, or to BEAT to keep the loop on the beats of the clock it was recorded to. With the cursor on SNAP, its L knob moves the whole loop a few milliseconds.
  • Turn SPEED for slower, faster or backwards (push R twice quickly to reverse it).
  • Hold A for 2 s to save the loop to the card, in LOOM/SPOOLS.
More about SPOOL

The next A press closes the loop and carries on overdubbing; after that A switches between overdubbing and just playing. B while recording closes the loop and plays. While recording, the picture grows from the left, 4 s across at first and zooming out whenever it reaches the edge. If memory is short (the other track is recording too), the loop may stop earlier, and "memory full" shows when nothing is left.

A saved loop goes to LOOM/SPOOLS named by session, for example Spool_S014_01 (see file names), together with its settings (START, END, FADE, LEVEL, SPEED). SHUTTLE's file stays as it is, but the next time you open SHUTTLE's file list, it opens in SPOOLS with the cursor on the new loop: push R to load it, and it plays the way you set it up.

WARP

Turns a loop, file or waveform into an instrument played from MIDI or CV; WEFT

WARP turns a sound into an instrument. It plays the other track's loop or file, or a single-cycle waveform, at the pitch of the MIDI notes you play, up to eight notes at once, or of pitch CVs and gates from the rack. Middle C plays the sound as it was recorded; every key above or below plays it faster and higher or slower and lower, like a tape at another speed, and a held note goes round the sound for as long as you hold the key.

Each note has its own envelope, and pressing a key harder makes it waver, like a violinist's vibrato. So a drum loop becomes a pitched texture, a recorded voice a choir, and a waveform from the card a plain synth voice.

How to play it

  • Turn SRC's L knob to pick the source: SPL the other track's SPOOL loop, SHU its SHUTTLE file, THR a waveform from the card.
  • On THR, push R and turn to step through the waves. Push R twice quickly for the file list; the wave under the cursor plays at once.
  • Play notes from a MIDI keyboard or sequencer. On page 3, MIDI picks the channel it listens to (OMNI: any).
  • To play it from the rack, set CV to the pitch input and GATE to the gate on page 3. Turn VOICE to POLY for four voices on TR 1–4, and pick their four pitch inputs with CV.
  • On page 2, shape each note with ATK, DEC, SUS and REL. Their L knobs bend the curves.
  • Turn VEL up and soft keys play quieter than hard ones.
  • Press a key hard for vibrato. WEFT sets its speed, and its L knob what wavers: pitch, level, tone or pan.
  • Press A (TRIG) to play the sound once at its own pitch. Hold B (PLAY) to hear it for as long as you hold: a quick way to try sounds without a keyboard.
More about WARP

The eight squares in the top line are WARP’s voices: a square fills while its note is held, by a key, a gate or B. Any quiet at the start and end of the sound is left out, so notes speak at once; the left-out parts are drawn dotted. A ninth note takes over the oldest one, fading it out quickly so nothing clicks. A note at full velocity is as loud as the sound was recorded. On the weave view WARP shows as WRP.

Where the sound comes from. On SRC, the L knob picks one of three sources:

  • SPL: the other track's SPOOL loop. Each time a recording or an overdub there ends, WARP takes the new loop over by itself, and notes that are still sounding play on into it. Record a loop on track A, then play it from a keyboard on track B.
  • SHU: the other track's SHUTTLE file, the loop section you set there (up to 16 s). It is read from the card once you stop moving the loop points.
  • THR: a thrum, a single-cycle waveform from the LOOM/THRUMS folder (see below). A fresh WARP starts here, on a random one.

The value shows the source and the track it takes from (SPL A, SHU A) or the thrum's name, short (SAW07). When the other track isn't on that source, the picture says so (SPOOL IS OFF, SHUTTLE IS OFF).

Browsing thrums. With SRC on THR, push R once to grab it, then turn R: it steps through the waves in the thrum's folder, one by one, A to Z. Push R twice quickly to open the file list and go to another folder. In WARP's list, the wave under the cursor plays as soon as the cursor rests on it, so hold a chord and turn: the notes you hold go on in each new wave, and you hear the folder go by. Push R on a wave or press Y to close the list; the wave you heard last stays.

WEFT: pressing harder

WARP stretches a sound across the keyboard like warp threads on a loom (see the weaving words): every key holds it at another pitch. WEFT is what your hand does while a key is down, the thread drawn across. Press a key hard and its note starts to waver, like a violinist's vibrato: nothing happens up to three quarters of the key's pressure, then the wobble grows with the pressure, up to a semitone either way when you press all the way down. The L knob on WEFT picks what wavers instead of the pitch: LVL pulses the level, down to silence at full pressure, a tremolo; CUT sweeps a filter, darker the harder you press, with a wah-like wobble; PAN swings the note between left and right. It eases in and out, and every note wobbles at its own slightly different pace, so a chord shimmers rather than moving in lockstep. WEFT sets how fast. DREAM

A whole piece of slow electronic music that plays by itself

DREAM is a whole piece of slow electronic music that plays by itself: a Drone (a chord over a held bass note, walking through the scale), two Sequences ticking in sixteenths, a gliding Lead that makes up its own phrases, and a soft Atmosphere of wind, surf or tape hiss. The piece moves through four sections, EMERGE, FLOW, SOLO and BRIDGE, with its own echo and plate reverb, and never plays the same way twice. Pick one of 16 styles, press A, and it starts.

You can play along. The white keys of a MIDI keyboard play the Lead in the piece's scale, and the Lead answers what you play. The black keys hold effects. Pressing harder shapes both. The same works from two CV inputs, so a sequencer or another module can play along too. DREAM's notes come out of the CV outputs and over MIDI as well, to play the rest of the rack or another synth.

How to play it

  • Press A to start the piece. Hold A for 2 s to stop it.
  • Press B for the next chord. Hold B for 2 s for a fresh piece in the same style.
  • Turn STYLE to pick one of the 16 styles. Past them are eight slots of your own, U1 to U8: hold B on one to keep what is playing there.
  • Turn TEMPO to set the speed. Its L knob set to SYNC follows LOOM's clock.
  • Turn ROOT for the key. Its L knob picks the scale.
  • On page 2, turn DRONE, SEQ, LEAD and SEQ2 for each layer's level. Their L knobs pick the voice, then move it an octave or two up or down.
  • Play the white keys of a MIDI keyboard to play the Lead. Play three notes or more and pause: the Lead takes them as a theme and answers.
  • Hold a black key for an effect: C# Take-off, D# Supernova, F# Twitter, G# Full, A# Shimmer.
  • Press a key harder to push it further: the Lead wavers and opens up, the effects grow.
  • Turn OUT to CV, MIDI or CV+MI to send DREAM’s notes out. Its L knob (CVIN) picks a pair of CV inputs, 1+3, 2+4, 5+7 or 6+8, to play the same from CV: the first input plays the white keys, the second the black keys.
More about DREAM

The piece. Each style brings its own key, tempo, voices, echo and pace. The Drone holds a chord and walks to the next one every few bars; B moves it on at the next bar. The two Sequences are short patterns in the scale that change a little as the piece goes on. The Lead glides between notes, with a vibrato that comes in late, and plays its ideas in pairs of phrases. The arc of four sections opens and closes the filters, brings layers in and out, and gives the Lead its solo. On the weave view DREAM shows as DRM.

The Sequences. They are kept sparse, the way a drummer keeps a groove: Sequence 1 sits on the beat (the one first, then the other beats), Sequence 2 between the beats, and Sequence 2 never plays on a sixteenth where Sequence 1 has just played, so the two answer each other instead of piling up. Over 16 bars each plays four blocks of four bars, every block a little different: a few soft ghost notes added in one, other notes taking their turn in the next. Sequence 2 runs through the blocks in reverse, busier where Sequence 1 is plainer. The fourth bar of each block of Sequence 1 has a pickup note into the next, and the sixteenth bar ends in a short fill. Hold G# (Full) to bring the Sequences' whole patterns back: the harder you press, the more notes, and pressed all the way down they play every step, short and plucked.

FIBRE

Granular: clouds of small grains from what comes in

FIBRE pulls the sound apart into grains: tiny slices of the recent past, each played back with its own length, pitch and place in the stereo field. Many grains at once make a cloud, from a soft shimmer around the sound to a glittering swarm that has little left of the original.

It keeps listening while it plays, so the cloud follows what you play. Freeze it, and it holds the sound it has and keeps scattering grains from it for as long as you like.

Page 3 turns it into a CLOUD: softer and thicker, each grain slightly out of tune with the others, like a choir made of your sound, with grains that play backwards or jump an octave or a fifth.

How to play it

DRAFT changes the pitch of a sound without changing its speed. It keeps the last four seconds of the track like a tape loop and plays them back in short, overlapping windows, so a voice can sing an octave lower at the same pace, or a chord shift up without hurrying.

SHEAR

Cuts the loop of SHUTTLE or SPOOL into slices and plays them anew

SHEAR cuts the loop playing in its track into slices and plays them back in a new order: forwards in step with the loop, backwards, shuffled, at random, from MIDI keys or one slice per trigger. It reads the loop of SHUTTLE (in memory, MEM) or SPOOL right where it is, so a change to the loop changes the slices at once. SHEAR is there only while SHUTTLE or SPOOL is the track’s source.

While the loop plays forwards, SHEAR rides along with its play head, so at MIX 50 the slices sit exactly on the beat with the loop itself. Slices can play backwards, roll like a drum fill, change pitch with or without changing their length, and send their own pitch and a trigger out to the rack.

How to play it

  • Put SHUTTLE (on MEM) or SPOOL in the track’s SOURCE slot, then turn L on the FIBRE slot in the weave view to SHR.
  • Turn SLICE for how many slices, 2 to 32. Its L knob picks how the cuts are spread: EUCL evenly over the grid, HITS on the loudest spots.
  • Turn ORDER: FWD plays the slices as they lie, BACK from the end, RAND at random.
  • Turn RATE left for slices that come less often (/2 to /8), right for more often (x2 to x8). Its L knob, SHUF, swaps that share of the slices around: press B (NEW, on the trigger page) for a new shuffle.
  • Turn PITCH to play the slices higher or lower. Its L knob picks TAPE (faster and higher, like a tape) or WARP (the slice keeps its length).
  • Set ORDER to KEYS and play MIDI keys: C3 plays the first slice, the next keys the next ones. Hold a key and its slice loops; hold several and they play as an arpeggio, in ARP’s order.
  • Set ORDER to TRIG, put a trigger on HIT (hold L), and put a CV on PICK as a modulator: each trigger plays the slice the CV points at, once.
  • On page 2, turn REVRS for backwards slices and ROLL for drum rolls; ROLL’s L knob picks 2, 3 or 4 hits.
  • Turn GATE down for short, choppy slices. Set OUT to a pair of CV outputs to play the rack along with the slices.
More about SHEAR

The loop and the grid. SHEAR cuts the loop between START and END, as SHUTTLE or SPOOL plays it. The slices sit on a grid of 8, 16 or 32 steps (the smallest that holds them all); with 8, 16 or 32 slices each slice is one step, other counts spread over the grid as EUCL or HITS has them. Each slice lasts its steps. Without a loop to cut, the page says so: SHEAR needs SHUTTLE on MEM or SPOOL with a recording in the same track. Change the number of slices while it plays, and the new cuts start when the slice playing now has ended, so nothing jumps.

Only with SHUTTLE or SPOOL. SHEAR needs a loop to cut, so the FIBRE slot offers it only while the track’s source is SHUTTLE or SPOOL. Switch the source to anything else and SHEAR fades out, and the slot shows FIBRE, bypassed. Switch back to SHUTTLE or SPOOL and SHEAR returns with all its settings, playing again if it was playing before (unless you changed the slot in the meantime). After a restart the slot stays on FIBRE.

In time. While the loop plays forwards, SHEAR follows its play head, so slices and loop stay together, also when SPEED or SYNC changes the tempo. With the loop stopped, backwards or on PING, SHEAR runs on LOOM’s clock at the loop’s length instead. FWD at PITCH 0 and RATE AUTO plays the loop just as it is, without a seam; everything else is a variation on it.

KEYS. MIDI notes on any channel play the slices: C3 the first, each key up the next, round again after the last. A key held down loops its slice; the last key pressed wins. Two keys or more held play as an arpeggio at RATE’s pace, in ARP’s order. How hard you press counts a little (VELO). B, HIT, plays the slice PICK points at.

TRIG. Only HIT plays: each trigger on HIT plays one slice, once, and nothing plays on the clock. No trigger, no sound. PICK sets which slice; a modulator on PICK picks it from a CV, its range spread evenly over the slices, left to right: at +100 % the lowest voltage plays PICK’s slice and the highest the last one. Past the last slice it stays on the last. The slice the next trigger will play is framed on the picture. On the trigger page (hold L), HIT then offers only what makes sense as a trigger: the trigger inputs, MIDI maps and the modulators.

REED

48 resonant bands in a scale: a filter, or ringing chords

REED is a bank of 48 resonant bands, tuned to a note and a scale, like the reed of a loom with one thread in every slot. With a short decay it is a filter with a character of its own: a peak that can be gentle or sharp, and a slope that tilts the sound from dark to thin.

Turn the decay up and the bands start to ring on as pitches after every hit. Any sound turns into pitched ringing in the scale you pick: a drum loop becomes a chord, a stream of hits a melody.

How to play it

  • Turn CUT to place the peak on a note, and RESO to make it sharper.
  • Turn SLOPE to let the lows or the highs fall away.
  • Turn DCAY past the middle to make the bands ring. Its L knob on CHOKE stops the ring when the input stops.
  • On page 2, pick the notes in SCALE and move them all with PITCH.
  • For a melody, turn PITCH's L knob: each hit then rings one note of the scale. PENTA and about ±7 is a good start.
  • Press A (HIT) to strike the bands, so they ring with nothing coming in.

TWILL

Six bells tuned to a chord or scale

TWILL is six bells tuned to the notes of a chord or a scale. Only those notes ring, clean and glassy, whatever goes in: a drone turns into a chord, and every hit rings the bells like struck glass. Each bell is two filters on the same note, one that lets only that note in and one that sets how long it rings.

The bells can ring together as a chord or one after another as an arpeggio. They can walk through the chord, follow the key you play, or drift to richer notes of the same chord family on every hit. The tunings go from the piano's to pure intervals and the scales of gamelan and Indian music.

How to play it

  • Pick a chord or scale in SCALE, and the root in KEY. KEY's L knob on MIDI lets the keyboard set the root.
  • Turn DIR for how many bells ring at once. Its L knob sets what each hit does: UP or DOWN walks the bells round, RAND jumps.
  • Turn SPRD to spread the bells wider over the chord. Its L knob picks which bells ring.
  • Turn ROTA to turn the bells through the chord into other voicings. Its L knob (MORPH) blends from one to the next.
  • Turn VARI to move some bells to nearby notes of the chord's family on every hit.
  • Turn DCAY for how long they ring. Low settings let your sound through, shaped by the six notes.
  • Press A (HIT) to strike the bells. Put a TREADLE or a gate on HIT (the trigger page) to play them in rhythm.
More about TWILL

On the weave view TWILL shows as TWL.

Chords and arpeggios. The six bells sit on notes of the SCALE (a chord or a scale), SPRD places apart, and ROTA turns them through it. DIR sets how many of them ring, SPRD's L knob which. They ring together; DIR's mode (L knob) sets what each hit does to them: UP moves the whole pattern a place up round the six (2 6 becomes 3 1), DOWN a place down, RAND somewhere else, OFF keeps it still. The bells a hit leaves behind keep ringing at their own pitch while the new ones sound, so the picture can show more lines than are chosen. TEXTURE

Drive, wavefolding, crushing, tilt EQ and noise

TEXTURE is the dirt and colour of the chain: drive, wavefolding, bit and sample-rate crushing, a tilt EQ, and noise that follows the input's level. It goes from a little warmth to a broken, metallic crunch, and its noise can add hiss, breath or the crackle of an old record that comes and goes with the sound.

DRIVE, FOLD and TILT even out the level as they work, so turning them up changes the sound, not the volume. A fresh TEXTURE starts clean, with everything at 0. Its slot can hold HEDDLE instead.

How to play it

  • Turn DRIVE up for saturation, FOLD for West Coast wavefolding: the brighter, vocal timbre of a folded sine. It works best on simple sounds, such as WARP on a SINE thrum.
  • Turn CRSH to crush the sound. Its L knob picks BIT (steps), REDUX (a lower sample rate) or BOTH.
  • Turn TILT to lift the lows or the highs. Its L knob sets where it turns.
  • On page 2, turn NOICE up to add noise to the loud parts. Its L knob picks WHITE, PINK or SPEC (crackle), and sets N.TON to suit it.
  • N.DCY sets how long the noise hangs on after each hit, N.TON its colour, MIX the balance with the dry sound.
  • Press A (NEW) for new variations at once. A gate on NOIS (the trigger page) plays a burst of noise, like a snare.
More about TEXTURE

HEDDLE

Frequency shifter and phaser

HEDDLE lifts a sound up or pushes it down, as the heddles of a loom lift and lower the threads. As a frequency shifter it moves every frequency by the same number of Hertz, so the harmonics stop fitting together: a plain note turns bell-like and metallic, large shifts clang like a ring modulator, and tiny shifts make a slow, rolling phasing.

As an endless phaser it leaves the pitch alone and sweeps a row of notches up or down through the sound for ever, the barber-pole sweep that never turns round. It works best on rich sounds: pads, noise, chords, distorted sources. HEDDLE goes in TEXTURE's slot instead of TEXTURE.

How to play it

  • In the weave view, turn the L knob on TEXTURE's slot to pick HEDDLE.
  • On the first setting, turn the L knob to pick SHIFT or PHASE, free or on the clock (SHCLK, PHCLK).
  • Turn it right of the middle to go up, left to go down. A tiny amount gives a slow sweep, a big one a clang.
  • For SHIFT, SIDE sets up, down, or BOTH for a wide stereo picture. For PHASE, STAGE sets how many notches.
  • Turn FDBK (SHIFT) for spirals, or RESO (PHASE) for sharp, whistling notches.
  • Turn the L knobs for variations: a new side, stage count or amount on every beat.
  • Press A (NEW) for new variations at once. A gate on FLIP (the trigger page) turns the shift the other way.
More about HEDDLE
  • SHIFT, a frequency shifter after Harald Bode's. Every frequency in the sound moves by the same number of Hertz, up or down. Shift by +100 Hz and 100 Hz becomes 200, 200 becomes 300, 300 becomes 400: the harmonics stop fitting together, so even a plain note turns bell-like and metallic. Large shifts clang like a ring modulator; with FDBK the sound spirals.
  • PHASE, an endless phaser. It leaves the pitch alone and cuts a row of narrow notches into the sound, then slides them up or down for ever: the barber-pole sweep that never turns round. Three phasers run at once, a third of the way apart, each fading in at the bottom and out at the top, so the movement never jumps. It works best on rich sounds: pads, noise, chords, distorted sources.
  • SHCLK and PHCLK are the same two on the clock: the speed is a clock note value instead of Hz or octaves a second.

In short: SHIFT bends the notes, PHASE sweeps the tone. HEDDLE starts with MIX at 100 and SHIFT at 0, which leaves the sound as it is: turn SHIFT away from the middle to hear it.

HEDDLE keeps the level it gets, each side on its own: the ringing of FDBK and RESO doesn't make the sound louder, and PHASE's notches don't make it quieter. Switching between SHIFT and PHASE fades over a few milliseconds, so it doesn't click.

REPEAT

Tape-style stereo delay and reverb (GLINT)

REPEAT is a tape-style stereo delay: every sound comes back after the delay time, and goes round again as long as the feedback lets it. On each pass the repeats are darkened or thinned, smeared across the stereo field and gently saturated, so a long trail wears down like an old tape loop instead of staying clean. More playback heads can join before the main echo, in one of thirteen patterns, from a dotted eighth or a triplet to a slapback, a clave or the uneven heads of an old tape echo.

Page 2 is a reverb built from eight lines that feed into each other, some of them slowly wobbling so the tail never rings metallic. It reaches from a small room to a sixteen second cloud, with soft, dark walls or hard, bright ones. It can also shimmer: the tail is pitched and sent back into itself, so it climbs with every pass into a halo above the sound, in one of twenty recipes. Turning through the recipes, LOOM waits until the knob rests (about a quarter of a second) and then blends into the new one, so the recipes passed on the way don't cut in.

How to play it

  • Turn TIME to space the echoes. Turn its L knob to CLOCK and the time locks to note values of the clock.
  • Turn FDBK up for longer trails: around 50 a few repeats, from 90 up almost endless. Turn its L knob to pick the playback heads: ONE for a plain delay, DOTTD, TRIPL, CLAVE, TAPE and more for a rhythm of echoes.
  • SPRD widens the echoes: PHASE lets the right side lag behind, PING bounces them from side to side, DIFF melts them into a wash.
  • Press A (FRZ) to hold what is in the delay and play over it; the top line blinks FREEZE meanwhile. Press it again to let go.
  • Press B to switch off the part whose page you are on. What is still sounding rings out.
  • On page 2, turn ROOM for the size and the length of the tail, WALL for dark and dense or bright and reflective, and PRE for a gap that keeps the attack clear.
  • For a shimmer, turn ROOM's L knob right and turn GLINT up, then turn WALL's L knob right and pick a preset in PRSET.
More about REPEAT

The delay picture is a time line from 0 at the left to 6 s at the right. The filled bars are the heads, the thin lines after them the repeats, as loud as you hear them: each round lower by what FDBK keeps, and gone once a repeat is too quiet to hear. Turn TIME and the echoes move closer together or further apart. With SPRD up, an outlined bar shows the right side: on top of the left one at first, moving out a pixel at a time as SPRD goes up.

The dry sound passes through untouched, and the echoes or the reverb tail already sounding ring out instead of stopping. Press B again to switch it back on. So you can run only the reverb, only the delay, or both.

GLINT turns the reverb into a shimmering verb: the tail is pitched and fed back into the reverb, so it climbs with every pass into a bright halo above the sound. ROOM and WALL each hold two things, and the L knob switches between them: turn it right and ROOM becomes GLINT (how much shimmer) and WALL becomes PRSET (which kind), turn it left for ROOM and WALL again. The thin line over the name shows which half you are on, and the page stays that way until you switch back. The R knob always changes what is showing. There are twenty presets (OCT to start). Each one sets the pitch on the left and on the right (slightly apart, so the tail slowly beats and spreads wide), how long the tail waits before it is pitched again (so the climb comes in steps), how much of its lows, mids and highs climb, and how strongly it goes round. It dies away when the sound stops. In the picture, GLINT shows as a halo of dots round the tail and dark sparkles in it, drifting up.

CLOTH

Level, pan and mix of each track, compressors, EQ; recording (BOLT)

CLOTH is the end of every track: what comes off the loom. It sets how loud the track is, where it sits between left and right, and how much of the effects you hear against the dry source. Its picture is a live spectrum analyser of the track's output.

Page 2 is a three-band EQ with a compressor in each band, so the lows, mids and highs can each be lifted, cut or held together on their own, and PLUSH, which squashes the whole track and brings it back up: denser, louder, more intense. A limiter behind it keeps the tracks from ever clipping. CLOTH is also where you record the whole set to the card.

How to play it

  • Turn MIX from the dry source to the whole chain.
  • Turn LEVEL for the track's volume (100 leaves it as it is) and PAN to place it.
  • Push R on MUTE to fade the track out and back in.
  • On page 2, turn LOW, MID and HIGH to lift or cut each band. Turn their L knobs to squash a band and hold it together.
  • Turn PLUSH up to squash the whole track and bring it up, for more intensity. The box above it draws the curve, with a dot for the sound coming in. Turn its L knob to switch to SPLIT, then R moves where the bands divide, and the box shows the split points.
  • Hold A for 1 s to start recording the whole set (a BOLT), and again to stop. A gate on DUCK (the trigger page) dips the track for a moment, like a kick pumping it.

Each band's compressor has its own speed: slow on the lows, so bass notes don't wobble, quicker on the mids, and fast on the highs, so it catches the snap of a hit. With everything off and the levels at 0 dB, the sound passes through untouched. Turn the levels with the compressors off and it is a plain three-band EQ.

LOOM keeps the sound from clipping. If band boosts or a high LEVEL would push a track, or both tracks together, past the top, a limiter turns it down just enough and lets it back up within about 150 ms. Pushed very hard, you may hear the level breathe: then turn LEVEL or the boosts down a little.

Recording the whole set (BOLT)

LOOM can record everything it plays to the SD card, for as long as you like: a whole performance, both tracks after their CLOTH pages, exactly as it leaves the module's output jacks. The quick listen from the file lists is left out, so checking files during a set doesn't end up on the recording.

  • On either track's CLOTH page, hold A for 1 s to start, and hold it again to stop. While you hold, REC (or STOP) fills up at the top of the page. A short press does nothing, so a take can't start or end by accident. It always records both tracks. A gate or a MIDI note can start and stop it too, at once (hold the L knob on the page to pick one).
  • While it records, the weave view shows a blinking dot and the running time in place of the slot name. CLOTH's own header shows the time too, unless the track is muted or bypassed.
  • Each take is saved in LOOM/BOLTS, for example Bolt_S014_02 (see file names below). Takes are stereo, 16-bit, 48 kHz. That is about 11 MB a minute, or 690 MB an hour. A single take stops by itself after about 6 hours.
  • The file is brought up to date every 5 seconds. If the power goes off during a take, everything up to the last few seconds is kept.
  • Opening card mode, or switching away from LOOM, ends a running take properly.

File names

Everything LOOM saves to the card, a SPOOL loop or a recording of the whole set (a BOLT), is named by session: Bolt_S014_02 is the second thing saved in session 14. A session is one stretch between switching on and off, and it only counts once something is saved in it, so the numbers stay small and have no gaps.

Loops and takes share the count, so everything from one evening sits together, in the order you made it, in LOOM/SPOOLS and LOOM/BOLTS.

How to play it

  • Hold A for 1 s on either CLOTH page to start a recording, and again to stop.
  • Open SHUTTLE's file list and go into BOLTS to find your takes.
  • Press B on a take for a quick listen, push R to load it into SHUTTLE and play it through the chain again.
  • Copy takes to a computer in card mode.
More about File names

Names don't carry a date, because the module has no clock battery and doesn't know the date after it has been switched off.

While it records, BOLT holds back about 1 MB of memory to ride over the moments when the card pauses. SPOOL and SHUTTLE have that much less while a take runs. If the card can't keep up (a slow or very old card), SLOW blinks in place of the time and a little audio is lost. If a take can't start, CLOTH's header says why for 3 seconds: NO CARD or NO MEM.

Modulators

Eight sources of movement: four per track, four shared

Each track has four modulators of its own, A to D, and both tracks share four more, E to H. A modulator is a source of movement: a wave (WAVE), an envelope (ADSR), a random pattern (RANDOM), a follower of the level (FOLLOW), a drum pattern (TREADLE) or a set of clock dividers (TABBY). They move settings, strike devices' actions and come out of the eight CV jacks.

The MOD view shows them as a row of boxes, each drawing what its modulator puts out, newest on the right. Page 1 is the track's own, page 2 the shared ones, page 3 the CV outputs and page 4 the MIDI maps.

How to play it

  • Press X for the MOD view, and again to go back.
  • Turn R to move between the boxes and pages.
  • Turn L on a box to change its modulator type.
  • Push R to open a modulator's page.
  • On a running WAVE, TABBY or TREADLE, hold A for 2 s to stop it, press A to run it again, B to start it over.
  • Push L on a box, then turn L, to pick which of a TABBY's or TREADLE's outputs it sends.
More about Modulators
  • R turn moves between the boxes and on to the next page.
  • L turn changes the modulator type in the box: none, ENV (ADSR), FOL (follower), RND (random), WAV (LFO), TRD (TREADLE, drum patterns), TABBY (clock dividers).
  • R push opens it. Its page works like any device page, including modulating its own settings.
  • Run and stop (WAVE, TABBY, TREADLE): on its page, hold A for 2 seconds to stop it, as on SHUTTLE, and press A to run it again. The top bar shows a play mark while it runs, a square while it is stopped. Stopped, WAVE holds where it is and TABBY and TREADLE play no hits. B sends it back to the start.
  • L push, then L turn, picks what the box sends. Most modulators have one output, but TABBY and TREADLE have several. On their own box, this picks which of their outputs it sends. Any other box can instead send one of their outputs. The bottom line shows the choice, lit while you turn; L push again (or Y) ends it. See Several outputs.

Modulator types

WAVE, RANDOM, ADSR, FOLLOW, TREADLE drums and TABBY dividers

Six kinds of modulator. WAVE is a cycling wave, free or on the clock. RANDOM makes new values, or repeating patterns that slowly change, and can play them as notes of a scale. ADSR is an envelope with its own curves, played by a gate, a note or another modulator, once or looping. FOLLOW traces how loud the track is.

TREADLE plays drum grooves and TABBY divides a clock into rhythms; both have several outputs and are described below. Every modulator's page works like a device page, so its settings can be modulated too.

How to play it

  • Turn L on a box in the MOD view to pick the type, then push R to open it.
  • On WAVE and RANDOM, turn RATE's L knob to CLOCK to lock the speed to note values.
  • On ADSR, turn the L knobs of ATT, DEC and REL to bend each stage. On page 2, LOOP makes it cycle and TIME stretches all three.
  • On FOLLOW, set RISE and FALL for how fast it follows, and GAIN for how high it reaches.
  • Press A and B for the modulator's actions: run, trigger, hold, reset.

TREADLE: drum patterns

On a loom, the treadling is the order in which the treadles are pressed: the rhythm of the weave. TREADLE plays drum grooves, each 32 steps (two bars of sixteenths) long, with a low part (bass drum), a mid part (snare) and a high part (hi-hats). All three play at once, each on its own output, as 10 V triggers.

It knows eight styles, from rock and funk to house, breakbeats, latin and dub, four beats each, plus 25 more. Each part can be thinned out or filled in, given its own length to drift against the others, and varied the way a drummer would, with ghost notes, pickups, fills and swing.

How to play it

  • On page 2 (GROOVE), pick a STYLE and a BEAT in it.
  • On page 1 (FILL), turn LOW, MID and HIGH: low plays only the main hits, high fills every step.
  • Turn their L knobs to give a part its own length, so it drifts against the others.
  • Turn CHAOS right for drummer figures, left to thin the groove out. Its L knob sets the swing.
  • Turn PLAY for backwards, back and forth, or drunk.
  • To play drums, send LOW, MID and HIGH to three CV outputs. To play TWILL or BEATER, put the TREADLE's MOD on their HIT.
More about TREADLE
  • STYLE (page 2, GROOVE) picks the kind of groove, and BEAT one of four in it:
    • ROCK: BEAT (the plain rock beat), PUSH (the kick pushing ahead), DRIVE (driving eighths), SLOW (half time).
    • FUNK: JB (a busy funk beat with ghost notes), GHOST (ghost notes on the snare), SYNC (syncopated kick), LINE (kick, snare and hi-hat taking turns).
    • HIPHP: BOOM (boom bap), LAZY (laid back), TRAP (half time with hi-hat rolls), BOUNC (a bouncing kick).
    • HOUSE: FOUR (four on the floor, hi-hat off the beat), GARAG (two-step garage), TECHN (techno, sixteenth hi-hats), DISCO.
    • BREAK: AMEN, FUNKY, THINK (the classic breakbeats), DNB (drum and bass two-step).
    • LATIN: BOSSA (bossa nova), SAMBA, CLAVE (the son clave on the snare), DEMBW (dembow).
    • DUB: 1DROP (one drop), STEPR (steppers), ROCKR (rockers), SKA.
    • MAP: 25 more patterns, numbered 1 to 25, from all over the place: less of a style, more of a surprise.
    Each beat has its main hits, some extra ones and the steps in between: LOW, MID and HIGH bring them in in that order, one step at a time and evenly. Up to 50 they build the beat as written, the strongest hits first; from 50 to 100 the extras come in, then the steps in between, until every step plays.
  • LOW, MID and HIGH (page 1, FILL) set how busy each part is: low, only its strongest steps play; high, almost all of them.
  • Their L knob sets how many steps the part has, 1 to 32 (LEN12 …; the bar over the name grows with it). Parts of different lengths drift against each other and meet again: a 12-step bass under a 7-step snare.
  • PLAY sets the direction for all three: FWD, REV (backwards), PING (there and back) or DRUNK (each step one forwards or one back, at random).
  • A new length counts at once and shows at once, but the part doesn't jump: it plays on from the step it is on and turns at the new end when it gets there. Dial a 32-step part down to 25 at step 10, and it plays on to 25 and starts again; dial it down below where it is, and it starts again on the next step.
  • A new PLAY waits until the part has finished its round: FWD after its last step, REV after its first, PING once it is back at the start, DRUNK after as many steps as the part is long.
  • CHAOS (−32 to +32) varies the groove the way a drummer would. LOW, MID and HIGH still set how busy each part is; CHAOS only varies it, so a part with no steps gets no variation.
    • Below 0 it thins the groove out: at −16 about half of the steps the part plays, at −32 all of them. The sixteenths in between go first, then the eighths, then the beats, and the first beat of the bar last. Accents, and the snare's backbeat, hold on longer.
    • Above 0 it plays round the groove, in the little figures drummers use: at +16 half as many steps again as the part plays, at +32 as many again. The kick (LOW) adds pickups into its hits, a push on the "and", a double into its next hit or a "ba-dum" after one, and stays off the snare. The snare (MID) adds ghost notes just before or after its hits and two-note drags into them, and ghost notes on the "e" and "a" between them. The hi-hat (HIGH) adds an "and", an "and-a" pair, a skip on the "a" or a quick run of three.
    • A variation holds for two run throughs before a new one comes, so the groove settles instead of changing all the time. When you turn CHAOS, a new one comes at once.
    • At the end of every second run through, the snare may play a short fill in the last beat (the higher CHAOS, the more often), and the hi-hat stops for it.
    • The L knob sets the swing: SW50 is straight, higher values play every second step later, up to SW74, a heavy shuffle. The bar over CHAOS grows with it.
    • The picture shows the steps left out in this run hollow and the added ones as short bars.
  • Each hit is a short pulse at full height (10 V on a CV output), as triggers on Buchla-format and Serge-format modules expect. The pattern's strong steps (its accents) also go to their own outputs: to play them louder, let an ACC output drive a level.
  • Outputs: LOW, MID and HIGH, then ACC.L, ACC.M and ACC.H, which send only that part's accents (full height).
  • The picture shows the three parts in rows, LOW at the top, each as long as it is: a bar for every step that plays (accents the full row), a dot for the others, and a line under the step playing.

Striking a device. Any modulator can strike a device's actions: on the trigger page (hold L), the sources go on after the jacks and MIDI with MOD A to MOD H. A modulator strikes each time its output rises past half way. Set TWILL's HIT to the TREADLE's MOD and the pattern plays TWILL's bells; ADSR's TRIG and RANDOM's CLOCK can take them too.

A whole kit. Put a TREADLE in a box, then let two more boxes send its MID and HIGH (see Several outputs). On three CV outputs, that plays three drums from one pattern: every hit a 10 V trigger. For accents, send ACC.L, ACC.M or ACC.H to a level on the same drum.

TABBY: clock dividers

Tabby is the plainest weave there is: one thread over, the next under, one against the other. TABBY plays two rhythms against each other the same way. RATE ticks, and two balls hop along the ticks: ball A lands every DIVA ticks, ball B every DIVB (3 against 4, 7 against 5), and every landing is a hit. Eight fixed dividers give the straight and triplet values on top.

CHANC adds a third voice that plays now and then between the hits, and a press of B plays a roll that starts fast and slows down into the next landing, like a snare fill. A trigger can start and stop it, or run it while a gate is held. Its outputs go to settings, devices' actions and the CV jacks, so one TABBY can clock a whole patch.

How to play it

  • Turn RATE for the tick, free or (L knob on CLOCK) a note value.
  • Turn DIVA and DIVB for how many ticks each ball hops. Their L knobs keep the count to a set: odd, prime, Fibonacci and so on.
  • Turn CHANC up for a third voice on the CHANC output. Its L knob picks where it may play: GAPS, APEX, LEAD or RICO.
  • Put the cursor on ROLL and press B for a roll. Its L knob picks who rolls: ROLL (both balls), ROL A or ROL B. Anywhere else, B lines the balls up again.
  • On page 2, GATE sets how long each hit lasts. Give START an input: a pulse starts and stops TABBY, or with its L knob on RUN it runs while the gate is high.
More about TABBY
  • DIVA and DIVB each strike every so-many ticks (DIVB can be OFF).
  • Their L knob keeps the count to a set of numbers: ALL (1 to 144), ODD, EVEN, PRIME (2, 3, 5, 7, 11 …), TRIP (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24 … straight and triplet values), FIB (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21 …) or 2^N (1, 2, 4, 8, 16 …). The R knob then steps from one member of the set to the next, and a modulator on the setting only lands on members too. Once a set is chosen, it shows as the setting's name.
  • Changes wait for the landing. A divider changed while it runs takes over when its ball next lands, so the hop in the air always finishes as it began. RESET, B, or the clock starting lines everything up: a ball in the air drops straight down, and both start together on the next tick.
  • Every landing is a hit, on A, on B and together on A+B.
  • CHANC is a third voice made from the clock and the balls. Its value is the chance that a tick it may play really plays (0: silent). All four ways run at once, each on its own output (GAPS, APEX, LEAD, RICO); the L knob picks which one the CHANC output sends:
    • GAPS: the ticks where no ball lands, so it fills the holes.
    • APEX: a ball at the top of its hop, half way between its landings.
    • LEAD: likelier the closer a ball comes to landing, like pick-up notes into the hit.
    • RICO: between landings that come close together, a rattle where A and B nearly meet.
    The picture on the right shows all four over the last eight ticks, the one picked in bold.
  • Rolls. ROLL sets the size of a roll (1 to 64). About a third of its hits come every half tick, then every three quarters of a tick, and the last a whole tick later, so it slows down like a snare fill. It is timed to end on the rolling ball’s next landing (with ROLL on both, A’s); when that is too close, it ends on the landing after. A roll plays at TABBY’s own tick, so it always sits in the tempo. The rolling ball’s own hits rest during the roll while its count runs on, so it carries on where it would have been.
  • START: with an input set, its L knob picks START, where each pulse starts TABBY from the top or stops it (the name reads what the next pulse will do, START or STOP), or RUN, where it runs while the input is high. With none, it runs until you hold A.
  • GATE sets how long each hit lasts, 5 ms to 1 s, for short triggers or longer gates on a CV output. A hit never lasts longer than most of a tick, so hits stay apart; at fast rates, GATE shows the length it really plays.
  • Outputs: A+B (A full height, B lower, at 6 V on a CV output), A and B (each ball’s landings), CLOCK (every tick), CHANC (the way its L knob picks), the fixed /2, /3, /4, /6 and /8, and GAPS, APEX, LEAD and RICO, each CHANC way on its own.
  • The picture: the stones along the bottom are the ticks, the dotted ones still to come. The balls stay where they are while the stones move under them, and the dotted line behind each ball is the way it really flew. A spark marks where A and B landed together. At slow tempos the ticks spread wider, so the balls still move smoothly.

Several outputs

TABBY and TREADLE have several outputs, while a box in the MOD view sends one. Any other box can send one of their outputs instead of running a modulator of its own, so one TREADLE can play a whole kit on three jacks, and one TABBY can clock several devices at different speeds.

How to play it

  • In the MOD view, push L on a box, then turn L: its own outputs come first, then those of the other boxes.
  • Push L again (or press Y) to confirm.
  • Turn L without the push to bring the box's own modulator back.
More about Several outputs
  • First come the outputs of its own modulator. On a TABBY box: A+B, A, B, CLOCK and so on.
  • Then the outputs of the other boxes that have several. The box then runs nothing of its own and sends that output; it shows the box's letter and the output's name, lit on each hit. The bottom line says, for example, from A /4.
  • A to D can take outputs from this track's A to D and from E to H; E to H only from E to H, as they are shared by both tracks.
  • R push on such a box opens the modulator it takes from. Turning L (without the push) brings the box's own modulator back.

Everything that listens to a box gets what it sends: a setting's MOD1 and MOD2, a trigger page, ADSR's TRIG, RANDOM's CLOCK, and the CV outputs. So one TABBY can clock several devices, and one TREADLE can play a whole kit.

RANDOM's QUANT: random notes

QUANT puts RANDOM's steps on the notes of a scale. The R knob picks the scale: off, CHROM (all twelve notes), MAJOR, MINOR, PENTA, mPENT (minor pentatonic), BLUES, FIFTH (root and fifth), WHOLE (whole tones), and the microtonal ones also found in TWILL: QUART, 17ET, 19ET, SLEND, PELOG, BOHLN, SHRUT and HARMO. The L knob picks the root note, C to B.

  • RANDOM's whole range is four octaves. Modulate a PITCH setting with it as u at +100 % and every step lands on a note of the scale. With b, use +50 % to stay on the notes.
  • AMNT makes the range smaller, OFFS moves it up. With SMTH up, it glides from note to note.
  • On a CV output, a RANDOM with QUANT on comes out as pitch: 1.2 V per octave on the Buchla-format module, 1 V per octave on the Serge and Eurorack formats. The module knows which one it is, so there is nothing to set. Patch it into an oscillator and it plays in tune, over four octaves from 0 V.

Clock

One clock for everything that keeps time: tempo, MIDI clock, triggers

One clock runs everything in LOOM that keeps time: synced delays and modulators, SHUTTLE on SYNC, TREADLE and TABBY, the variations that move on every beat. It runs on its own tempo, or follows MIDI clock or pulses at a trigger or CV input from your sequencer.

A small play triangle or stop square in the top line shows whether it runs. Synced modulators hold while it is stopped and start again together with it.

How to play it

  • Press Z to start or stop the clock.
  • Hold Z to open the clock page; the page changes while you still hold it. Hold Z again, or press Y, to go back.
  • Turn BPM for the tempo: slowly in 0.1 steps, faster in bigger ones. Or press A (TAP) in time: three presses set it, and two more are enough to change it again.
  • Turn the L knob on BPM to a CV input (CV1 to CV8) to play the tempo with a voltage: 0 to 10 V move it up from the BPM you set.
  • Set Source to MIDI or a trigger input to follow your sequencer, and PPQN to how many pulses make a beat.
  • Set Source to SHU A or SHU B (right after MIDI on the knob) to follow the sound file playing in that track's SHUTTLE.
  • Press B (RST) to start the bar again.
  • Set BootScreen for how long the boot picture stays when the module starts: off, 1 to 9 s, or until you move a knob or press a button.
  • Turn R past BootScreen to the bottom row: TEST, CALIBRATE and FLASH. Turn R to pick, push R to open (see Test, calibration and updates).
More about Clock

Following MIDI clock. Set to MIDI, the clock follows a computer, drum machine or sequencer sending MIDI clock. Its beats lock to the incoming clock, so the two don’t drift apart, also at high tempos. START begins from beat one, STOP stops, and CONTINUE (and a song position, when the host sends one) picks up at the right beat, so LOOM stays in the bar when you start the host from the middle of a song. The tempo reading is steadied, so it doesn’t flicker. While the clock follows MIDI, a CV on BPM’s L knob has no effect.

CV outputs

Eight jacks sending modulators out to the rack

Eight jacks, A to H, send modulators out to the rest of the rack, 0 to 10 V. A wave becomes an LFO for a filter, an ADSR an envelope, a TREADLE's parts triggers for three drums. A RANDOM with QUANT on comes out as pitch, in tune at the module's own volts per octave.

They live on page 3 of the MOD view. By default A to D carry track A's modulators and E to H track B's.

How to play it

  • Turn R to pick a jack, push R to grab it, then turn R to pick its modulator.

MIDI maps

32 maps for knobs, notes and wheels of your MIDI controller

LOOM has 32 MIDI maps, M1 to M32. Each one listens to one thing from your MIDI controller, a CC knob, notes, velocity or the pitch bend wheel, and turns it into a source like a modulator. A map can move any setting, or press a device's actions on its trigger page.

They live on page 4 of the MOD view. Each map learns its control from your controller, so there is nothing to look up.

How to play it

  • Put the cursor on a setting, push L for MOD1 and hold X, then move a knob on your controller. That's all.
  • Or, on page 4, push R on a map to open it, press A to learn, B to clear.
  • In a map, set CHAN, TYPE and the LOW / HIGH range by hand.
More about MIDI maps

How to play it

  • Hold Y for 2 s in the weave view (or the MOD view) to save everything: SAVE STATE. LOOM starts like this next time.
  • Hold Y for 2 s on a device's page to save only that device: SAVE DEVICE. Handy when the rest of the set should come back as it was.
  • Push both knobs together (L+R) for the preset page, and turn R to pick a slot.
  • Hold the L knob to store into the slot, hold the R knob to recall it. A bar fills while you hold.
  • Push both knobs again, or press A or B, to go back to LOOM.
More about saving

What hold Y saves depends on where you are (not in a file list, where Y goes up a folder). In the weave view, the MOD view, the CV outputs and the MIDI maps it is SAVE STATE: everything, both tracks, every device, the modulators, the clock, the links and where you were. On a device's page (a modulator's and the clock's too) it is SAVE DEVICE: only that device's settings, while everything else stays as it was last saved. The box shows which while you hold, then STATE SAVED or DEVICE SAVED. (If a device has never been saved before, SAVE DEVICE saves everything once instead.)

Both go into one file, LOOM.BIN, on the module's internal flash. That file is what LOOM starts with every time you switch it on. Saving never touches the 30 preset slots.

Anything changed since the last save is gone when the power goes off, so hold Y before you switch off. Saving takes a moment in which the sound stops for about an eighth of a second, so save between pieces rather than in the middle of one.
More about presets

A preset is a snapshot of the whole setup, both tracks with every device, the modulators, the clock and the links. Storing one also notes which nodes are playing on each track, and the page shows that under the slot's number: A's nodes on the first line and B's on the second, in their order in the chain. Bypassed or empty nodes are left out, and so is CLOTH, which is always last. A slot stored with an older firmware shows recall to see it until you recall it once.

Recalling a slot replaces the setup that is playing and the saved start-up setup (LOOM.BIN), so after a recall LOOM also starts with that preset next time. Sounds and loops on the SD card (SHUTTLE files, THRUMs, SIDs) stay on the card; a preset only remembers which file each device plays.

To name a stored slot, turn L one step from the number to the top line, which then lights, and click L to type: L moves along the name, R picks the letter, an L click is done. The name then shows on top instead of PRESETS.

Turn L down to NEXT or LAST and turn R to give it a TR input (or off). A trigger on NEXT recalls the following slot, a trigger on LAST the one before. This works while LOOM plays normally, too, so a clock divider or a sequencer can change presets on the bar.

In a Buchla system, the preset page also has a MANAGER page, hidden until you hold A+B+X+Y for 3 s. Switched on, this module sends its stores and recalls over the 225e bus, so the other modules follow its preset changes.

Card mode

Copying sound files to and from the SD card over USB

Card mode connects the module's SD card to your computer over USB, so you can copy sound files onto it and get your loops and recordings off it, without taking the card out. LOOM pauses while it is open, and the module itself shows the card's contents, so you can listen to files and delete them right there.

How to play it

  • Connect the module with a USB cable, then hold Z and push R.
  • Open the card on the computer. On a Mac, use the free app OpenMTP.
  • Copy WAV files into the LOOM folder; SHUTTLE finds them there.
  • On the module, press B to listen to a file, hold X for 2 s to delete it.
  • Hold Z and push R again to go back to LOOM.
More about Card mode
  1. Connect the module to the computer with a USB cable.
  2. Hold Z and push R. LOOM saves its settings and pauses, and the sound stops.
  3. Open the card on the computer. On Windows and Linux it shows up like a phone or camera. On a Mac you need the free app OpenMTP.
  4. Copy sound files into the LOOM folder (16-bit WAV, mono or stereo). SHUTTLE finds them there. Loops saved from SPOOL are in LOOM/SPOOLS, recordings in LOOM/BOLTS, and single-cycle waveforms for WARP go in LOOM/THRUMS. LOOM makes these folders itself when they are missing, and fills a new THRUMS folder with its starter waveforms.
  5. When you're done, hold Z and push R again to go back to LOOM.
A SPOOL loop is only kept in memory. Opening card mode clears it, so hold A on SPOOL for 2 s to save it to the card first.

While it waits, the page also shows LOOM's version and build number, for example LOOM RC1 4704 (the clock page shows it too, also while the computer is connected). Until a program on the computer opens the card, the screen says waiting.... On a Mac, if OpenMTP doesn't see the card, the Mac's own photo import may have grabbed it first. The module then says connected anyway. Quit the photo import, then try OpenMTP again.

The module shows the card's contents too, starting in the LOOM folder, with folders first. The small .loom files that hold each sound file's settings are not listed; they stay with their sound file. New files appear within a couple of seconds after you copy them:

  • R turn moves through the list. R push opens a folder.
  • Y goes back up a folder (so does “..” at the top).
  • B listens to the selected sound file, as in SHUTTLE's file list: once through, straight from the card. Loops in SPOOLS play just their loop section, other files play whole. B again stops it. Hold B for AUTO: every file the cursor stops on plays by itself.
  • Hold X for 2 s to delete the selected file. A bar fills while you hold. Let go early and nothing happens. Deleting a sound file also deletes its saved settings.

Test, calibration and updates

Testing the jacks, calibrating, updating the firmware

Three things you do only now and then, all on the bottom row of the clock page: checking that every jack works, calibrating the CV inputs and outputs so a volt in and out is exactly a volt, and putting a new firmware on the module.

How to do it

  • Open the clock page (hold Z) and turn R past BootScreen to the bottom row. Turn R to pick TEST, CALIBRATE or FLASH, push R to open it.
  • Test: patch something into a jack and watch it on the screen; turn R for the next output pattern. Y goes back to the clock page.
  • Calibrate: push R on CALIBRATE and follow the steps (see below). Afterwards you are back on the clock page.
  • Update the firmware: push R on FLASH, and a box asks: R puts the module into flash mode, L goes back. Your settings are saved first, then the module waits for the Teensy Loader: open the new .hex file there. No program button needed.
More about TEST

The page shows the jacks as they are laid out on your module. On the Buchla module that is CV1 and CV3 on top, then CV2 and CV4, CV5 and CV7, CV6 and CV8; on the Serge module CV1 to CV4 on the left and CV5 to CV8 on the right. A CV input's number lights up when it is above 1.25 V, the height that counts as a gate. A TR circle fills while its input is high and stays filled for a moment after a short trigger, so you can see every pulse.

While TEST is open, it drives the eight outputs instead of the modulators; the sound keeps playing. R turns through five patterns, sized to your module:

OCTAVES gives you eight exact octaves at hand, to tune an oscillator or check another module's tracking. Y, or leaving the clock page, gives the outputs back to the modulators.

More about calibration

You need a voltmeter for the outputs and a known voltage for the inputs. R push goes to the next step, L push back (L turn walks through the octaves of an output, then the steps); on the first two pages L push leaves. Hold L on any page to leave straight away. Leaving never saves: the calibration stays as it was stored, and you are back on the clock page. Hold X or Y while turning R for bigger steps.

  1. Start fresh? YES starts from the default values, NO keeps the ones stored and lets you fine-tune them.
  2. Center display: shifts the picture if it sits off-centre.
  3. DAC A to H, low and high point: with the voltmeter on that CV output, turn R until it reads the voltage shown. Turning L moves through the octaves in between; holding B fills those in from the two points.
  4. ADC input 0V offset: nothing patched into the CV inputs, then hold B to take their zero.
  5. ADC octave +1 and +3: send the voltage shown into CV input 1 and hold B at each step. On the Buchla module that is 1.2 V and 3.6 V (1.2 V an octave), elsewhere 1 V and 3 V.
  6. Screen blank, then Save? It starts on NO, so pushing R straight through stores nothing: turn R to YES to store it all. Either way you are back on the clock page, and LOOM plays on.

The calibration is kept apart from your settings and presets, so loading or resetting those never touches it.
